Chapter 2 · Terroir

IV. Biodiversity Features of the Portuguese Varieties

In Portugal about 350 varieties are utilised for wine production.

The autochthonous Portuguese varieties are the result of over 2000 years of natural human selection. It is obvious, however, that Portugal has many of its varieties in common with Spain.

In addition to descendants of the natural Iberian gene pool (chorotype A.) in its regional expression, since antiquity there have been constant imports of immigrant varieties from other gene centres, initially from the east of the Mediterranean Sea (chorotype C and D), which were used as such or crossed with the autochthonous varieties. Later with EU accession, there was an invasion from northern EU countries of some of the modish varieties grown worldwide. With one exception (Alicante Bouschet has been present in Portugal for 130 years)), these varieties have little significance and make up approximately 1% of the national crop..

It is obvious that the Portuguese reference grape varieties (such as Arinto, Fernão Pires, Aragonez, Trincadeira, Touriga Franca and Touriga Nacional) described in Chapter 3 produce good wine almost everywhere. Other varieties (such as Avesso, Antão Vaz, Fonte Cal, Tinta Caiada, Moreto, Ramisco, Jaen, Tinto Cão,), only produce great wines in very specific sub-regions, making them regional specialities.

The most notable of the Portuguese autochthonous grape varieties is Touriga Nacional; thanks to its genetic predisposition, ishas a wide range of different aromatic flavour components, producing different types of wine depending on the terroir. In the Dão, for example, one can detect a strong mineral component and masculine strength, yet Touriga Nacional wines from the Alentejo are much softer and have almost feminine notes. In the Douro, the quality of Touriga Nacional varies according to the vineyard elevation too, with less desirable quality normally coming from higher elevations. To a certain extent, vineyard elevation as a general factor affecting quality is true for many of the reference grape varieties.

However, the attempt to precisely define a catalogue of regional terroir influence for Portugal's leading grape varieties according to regions and sub-regions proved to be problematic. When asked to define fixed rules for this, I received the following explanation from one of the wellknown university oenologists:

1) Nowadays, there are so many techniques available to winemakers and wines are handled so frequently in wineries that the effect of the place where the grapes are produced is often cancelled out.

2) Portugal's designations of origin are so heterogeneous that it is possible to make very different wines from one variety, with almost opposite styles. Making a Touriga Nacional in the Douro grown at 180 metres above sea level with a southern exposure is very different from making a wine from the same variety at 700 metres with a northern exposure.

Another particular problem will be that of climate change. On the one hand, the same effect that is already occurring within the European wine-growing countries will be experienced in the Portuguese wine regions and sub-regions. In Germany, grape varieties from France that used to ripen much too late can now be planted, and in Bordeaux, which has not changed its grape variety mix for 100 years, the Portuguese Touriga Nacional can now be planted: Varieties are migrating northwards in line with climate change.

The wine regions of Portugal in the interior and southern interior will face particular challenges posed by the ecological developments as a result of climate change, for there are no grape varieties for the production of high quality wine to the south of these regions, where the climate has historically been already too warm for grapevine cultivation. That is why Portugal needs scientific work on wine improvement by breeding. Because it is not possible to import heatand drought-tolerant cultivars, we need to improve existing varieties by new clonal selection with different selection descriptors and even introduce resistance genes from other grapevine species into current Portuguese reference varieties through breeding.

By clonal selection and new breeding, the Iberian Gene-pool must be adapted to the future climatic conditions. (see chapter. IV). A particular challenge posed by the ecological challenge of climate change will be to find a sustainable way of adapting our European vines to the cryptogamic fungal diseases, introduced from other continents. Thousands of interspecific genotypes will be tested until finding new Portuguese resistant varieties.
