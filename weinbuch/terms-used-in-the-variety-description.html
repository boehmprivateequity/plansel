Chapter 3 · The Portuguese grape varieties

Terms Used in the Variety Description

Winkler Index

The Winkler index is a system developed by professors Albert Winkler at UC Davis in the 1940s for identifying the capacity of an area for grape growing and winemaking by the cumulative number of heat hours by sun above 50°F (10°C) during the growing season from April 1 to October 31. This index is an important tool for determination of grape varietals best suited to the region’s climate.

Zone 1 (below 1370 h Fahrenheit) cool climate for best white and sparkling wines like Mosel Valley, Chablis and Champagne.

Zone2 (1370–1650 h) medium warm climate with well bodied red wines like Nappa valley, Bordeaux Northern Rhone Valley.

Zone 3 (1650–1930 h) medium warm climate for full bodied wines like Barossa Valley, Stellenbosch or southern Italy.

Zone 4 (1930–2200 h) warm climate like with high alcoholic wines like Central California, Douro, Alentejo, Castilla- Leon)

Zone 5 (over 2200 h) Xerez (E,), like Baixa California,

Magrep-countries: fortified wines, table grapes or Vines from heat tolerant varieties.

The pruning weight of the annually pruned woody shoots of a vine is an important factor for calculating the Ravaz index or crop load, i.e., the balance between fruit yield and vegetative growth. It is a straightforward calculation comparing the yield of the vine at the time of harvest with the pruning weight in the following dormant period (yield/pruning weight).

Bud fertility index: Relation between number of inflorescences and insertion height of the flower bud.

Sensitivity to abiotic factors. "Environmental stresses can trigger dynamic responses in grapevines, comprising photosynthesis, phenology, and physiology which affects grapevine growth and berry ripening, which, in turn, can cause severe losses regarding yield and the quality of vines".

(Source: springer.com/article/10.1007/s13593-018-0544).

The interaction between climate, terroir features, for grapevine stress, which represents and effective challenge for sustainable Mediterranean viticulture, require management strategies to protect the crop. Higher temperatures and water deficit effect the traditional viticulture techniques. Besides, by adjusting natural and human systems, three significant purposes can be achieved: (1) reduce the risk of damage, (2) develop the capacity to manage certain damages, and (3) find opportunities with climate change. Little research has been carried out on grapevine varietal resilience, and there is scant scientific understanding of varietal sensibility regarding (abiotic) interactions between environmental parameters and plant adaptation responses (Ollat and Touzard 2014). Climatic variables, such as heliothermic conditions over the growing cycle, temperature summation, rainfall, potential soil water balance over the growing cycle, night temperature during berry ripening or seasonal weather data, with other non-climate-related indicators (e.g., potential quality of grapes at harvest and management practices) improved winegrowers’ adaptive strategies, both in time and space (Tesic et al. 2002; Malheiro et al. 2010) must be studied in selective form for each region in the climatic border situation and grape-variety breeding must be systematically improved.

Berry colour change (veraison): Veraison (French: véraison) is “change of colour of the grape berries” and represents the transition from berry growth to berry ripening. After veraison, fruit acidity decreases due to degradation of malic acid in favour of tartaric acid. At the same time, hexose sugars are accumulated and herbaceous aromas are degraded to fruit aromas.

Incidence of coulure/millerandage: Millerandage is a ripening defect in the vine leading to partial abortion of the grapes. The bunch has a characteristic appearance, with sparse berries of all sizes and, in the case of the largest berries, at all stages of ripening, including a large number of very small, seedless berries.

Alcohol content: Besides ethyl alcohol (80–160 g/l), wine also contains up to 25 g/l glycerin. In addition, there are traces of other alcohols, even some methyl alcohol. The sum of all alcohols is indicated on the label in percent by volume. The alcohol determination of the wine takes place in different state phases and with different techniques. In the must, the specific gravity (sugar) is determined with the “spindle” and converted to the alcohol result. Alternatively, the sugar content is determined photometrically using the refractometer (spectrophotometer) via the refraction of light and the estimated alcohol content is expressed as ethanol in g/ml or % w/v. The direct alcohol determination with an alcohol meter (hydrometer) gives an alcohol content in vol%. For this purpose, the alcohol must be separated by distillation before determining the density.

Acidity: Acidity plays a decisive role in the quality of a wine. Acidity not only influences the taste of a wine, but also its structure, shelf life and overall perception. The vineyard soil, the climate, the state of ripeness at harvest and the choice of grape variety have a significant influence on the acidity of a wine.

Two main acids dominate: tartaric acid and malic acid. Malic acid, which is perceived as more aggressive, can be converted into the softer lactic acid through the so-called biological acid reduction, the malolactic fermentation. Furthermore, carbonic acid, citric acid and ascorbic acid determine the acidity of the wine, finally the added sulphureous acid has to be taken into account.

The acidity can be determined in g/l or by the pH value. The lower the pH value, the higher the tartaric acid, the more stable the wine. The higher the pH value, i.e. the lower the acidity, the more critical the microbiology and stability of the corresponding wine.

Phenols: Name for aromatic compounds that are similar to alcohols. They belong to the group of organic acids with low acidity. The phenols essentially influence the colour, the smell, the taste as well as the texture of the wine comprise a group of several hundred chemical substances from the substance group of polyphenols, including tannins, which are also contained in oak wood, such as lignin. They are also found as a component of grape skins, seeds and stems. The content of phenols in red wine is significantly higher than in white wine grapes. However, the finest, most aromatic phenolic compounds are found in the berry skins. The phenols are responsible for the characteristic astringent taste impression in red wines. The most important phenolic compounds in wine are the flavonoid groups with anthocyanins, catechin, quercetin and tannin. Phenolic compounds such as resveratrol (with antioxidant a positive health properties),”and vanillin (a particularly valued flavour property), are also included. Phenols are formed in the skin of the berry, which are therefore exposed by modern winemakers to the sun. As the wine ages and matures, astringent “short”phenolic components are polymerized, than passing the respective taste receptors of the tongue and giving the wine its velvety maturity.

Determination of TPI – Total Polyphenols Index: The polyphenols electronic spectra have an absorption peak at 280 nm. The photometric reading at 280 nm is then proportional to the amount of the polyphenols present in wine or must sample.

Anthocyanins: are water soluble flavonoid pigments that accumulate in grape cell vacuoles which contribute colour to wine depending on pH and complexing agents available. Anthocyanins are responsible for the red-purple colour of grapes and red wine colour. They are a type of polyphenol from the flavonoid group that is the red pigment found in grape skins and sometimes in the flesh.“Teinturier” is the name of the red grape that contains anthocyanins in the flesh leading to red-purple berries. The most prevalent anthocyanin in Vitis vinifera grapes is the malvidin-3-O-glucoside. Malvidin-3.5.diglucoside is said to be responsible for the off-colour of hybrid grapevines.

Aromatic profile: Aromatic compounds are usually located in both pulp and skin as free and bound glycosides. The concentration – odour threshold value – must be considered as the principle to estimate the contribution of each compound to aroma, although interactions. Compounds, present in the wine, such as acids, sugars, polyphenols, mineral substances play a role on the quality and degree of appreciation.

The wine aroma can be classified in different ways according to origin and biotechnological conditions (Bayonove et al., 1998): varietal, pre-fermentative, fermentative aroma and post-fermentative aroma.

The pre-fermentative aroma is developed during the phases prior to fermentation, from the harvest, transport, storage, destemming, crushing, sorting, pressing and maceration as the result of several enzymatic activities where distinct compounds may be produced and released into the must (Cordonnier and Bayonove, 1981).

The fermentative aroma, composed of several compounds from different families, such as esters, aldehydes, cetones, alcohols, volatile acids and volatile phenols, originate through microorganisms present in the medium during alcoholic and malolactic fermentation (Rapp and Mandery, 1986; Riberéau- Gayon et al., 2006). Currently, there are many strains of yeast in commerce, with various metabolic capacities more or less accentuated, thanks to the precursors present in the grapes, which are able to develop new compounds and through the selection of different strains, the winemaker is able to differentiate the final product and to develop a wine that complies with the needs of the market (Molina et al., 2009; Vilanova et al., 2012).

Finally, the post-fermentative aroma originates after fermentation due to several chemical reactions, which may occur during the wine conservation and ageing (Marais and Pool, 1980; Usseglio-Tomasset, 1983; Vilanova and Oliveira, 2012).
