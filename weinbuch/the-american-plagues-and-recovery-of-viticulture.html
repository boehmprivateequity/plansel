Chapter 1 · Brief overview of the history of viticulture in Portugal

The American Plagues and Recovery of Viticulture

North America and Asia are home to wild species that are less suited to making wine, but in the past, they were of interest as table grapes. With the import of viticultural material from the United States of America to Europe in the 19th century, harmful agents for European vines also arrived unnoticed:

• In 1851, powdery mildew (Uncinula necator) was detected in Portugal

• In the 1880s, the harmful insect called phylloxera (Dacty losphaira vitifolii), followed by

• by downy mildew (Plasmopara viticola)

(Source: J. Böhm, Atlas das Castas da Península Ibérica, p. 69–77).

While the insect was combated by using phylloxera-resistant American grapevine material as rootstock, the fungus continues to be controlled to this day through excessive chemical control, with more than ten treatments a year in some regions, causing ecological and agronomic impacts. The human population has quadrupled in less than a century (source: Population growth over the years/Our World in Data), with damaging climatic effects. It was acknowledged that professional agricultural techniques were extremely damaging to natural ecosystems and the biodiversity of fauna and flora.

The first reactions to the severe damage caused by the infestation of American pathogenic agents throughout Europe were not very successful.

Control of phylloxera with direct producer hybrids was successful, but the wines of these American varieties were hardly drinkable. The agronomic experiments with flooding the vineyards or treating the soil with sulfur carbonate were not sustainable. The technique of grafting had been known since Roman times and described by Cato, Virgil, Pliny and Columella (F. Shut 1885:179), and led to success with the use of phylloxera-resistant American varieties as rootstocks.

However, plant protection was more difficult in the case of the cryptogenic fungal invaders. The use of American grape varieties or hybrids of these varieties helped to prevent fungal infestation, but the grapes were not suita-

ity of the old varieties. This finally led to the prohibition of the interspecific varieties throughout Europe in the middle of the 20th century. The genetic proportion of non-vinifera blood in these varieties was simply too large. Since it was now known that sulphur helped to combat powdery mildew and that copper sulphate could also be used to ble to be transform into tasty wine. The must of these varieties had characteristics that did not correspond to the typical European grape variety. In particular, the foxy- tone of the American hybrids was not suitable for pleasureful human consummation. After the turn of the 20th century, wine production in Portugal dropped drastically down.

French breeders had begun to create new fungus-resistant varieties by backcrossing American with European vines. Although their quality was drinkable, it was nowhere near the qualcombat downy mildew, there was a way to plant European vines again.

The wine economy in Portugal quickly recovered and even led to surplus production in some regions from its original 7 million hl to 14 million finally. (Boletim do Banco Ultramarino 1966:33). An essential measure was the establishment of the cooperative system to ensure a fair distribution channel for the small-scale grape producer. The attempt to regulate the market through state intervention led to the creation of enormous storage capacities by the Junta Nacional de Vinho and the paid distillation of the demand exceeding wine quantities. This system worked with a certain success when the Portuguese state controlled its import borders well. This policy led to the collapse of the old system after the political change to open markets in the 1970s.

From the second half of the 19th century onwards, grapevine improvement through crossbreeding had a tradition in Europe, especially in France with interspecific breeding and in the eastern countries, from the Caucasus to the Balkans. In the 1930s and 1940s in Germany, with the ideology of the genetic superiority of privileged individuals of national blood, sexualised breeding was even an official strategy, and was maintained at a technical-scientific level after the Second World War. Today, in many countries, there are cultivars obtained through crossbreeding: in Germany, the white variety "Müller Thurgau" (1882, Geisenheim) and the red variety "Dornfelder" (1955, Weinsberg) are today, each in its own category, the second most planted grape variety in the country. On the other hand, France has made progress with the "Alicante Bouschet" grape variety (1855) and, more recently, the "Marselan" (Paul Truel, 1961), not to mention the interspecific hybridisation that has taken place over the last century. Even in Portugal, at the Higher Institute of Agronomy, there was a large-scale programme in favour of obtaining new varieties by crossbreeding. The "Dona Maria" table grape variety and the "Serra Nova" wine grape variety still bear witness to the sexual breeding activity today. For half a century Portuguese Wine production was protected against competition defined by particular socio-political circumstances.
