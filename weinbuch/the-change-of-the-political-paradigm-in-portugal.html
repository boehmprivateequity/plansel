Chapter 1 · Brief overview of the history of viticulture in Portugal

The Change of the Political Paradigm in Portugal

Political changes ushered in by the 1974 Carnation Revolution impacted the viticultural and wine industry during the next twenty-five years. The domestic market which had previously been shielded from competition from European Union wine producers saw these protections being sacrificed in favour of a competitiveness strategy based on the free market dynamics.

While Portugal had a high degree of scientific expertise in the different areas of grapevine science and research, the same was not true of related commercial practices. There was no professional representation in the form of associations which could exert influence over the dirigiste administrative measures of the former government’s agricultural policy.

Portugal's viticulture was very backward. The use of grafted vines was unusual. In most cases, even virus-infected rootstock vines were manually grafted with scions the following year. The scions for grafting were used without selection from viticulturist’s own vineyards, with the result that traditional quality grape varieties lost their yield potential, or were replaced by grafts of mass-production varieties from external, "advanced" grape-grafting personnel who brought them. No attention was paid to varietal purity in the vineyards. There was also little interest in the old varieties in terms of sales, as there was a network of state-sponsored winegrowers' cooperatives and regional wineries of the Junta Nacional do Vinho (the precursor body to the IVV – Instituto da Vinha e do Vinho, I.P.) and no great consideration was given to wine quality. The sales problem was alleviated by state-financed distillation and monopolized exports to the Portuguese colonies.

Wine production was similarly backward. Harvest temperatures were above the evaporation point of the wine flavours, and cellar hygiene during fermentation and storage was very poor. Typical of this was that off-flavours such as Brettanomyces notes in the wine were even perceived by many oenologists as a typical positive quality characteristic. Subcontracted independent oenologists selected whichwines to be commercialized in the cellar and then had their own special techniques for making drinkable wines. The rest was "marketed" via the state distillery. The only regulatory institution at the time was the Junta Nacional do Vinho, which was responsible only for wine. Apart from a few export-orientated wineries and the major exception of Port wine production in the Douro Valley and some Vinho Verde wineries, there was little targeted quality policy. Portuguese viticulture had significant problems in that outside of Portugal besides the Port wine, only vinho verde and rosé-wines had a certain reputation for quality wine. Portuguese wine production dropped from 380,000 hectares down to the half that eventually, as a result of the mighty winds of change which swept through the market.
